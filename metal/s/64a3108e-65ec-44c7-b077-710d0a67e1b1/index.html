ConfirmedWhat the chart is really showing

This is a battery-powered iPad that sleeps between punches, connected to an access point that offers it both a 2.4 GHz and a 5 GHz radio. Two normal behaviours make the chart look far busier than the service actually is:

  • Sleep and wake. The screen sleeps, Wi-Fi drops, someone walks up to clock in, it rejoins. That happens around twenty times a day.
  • Band steering. Each time it joins, it lands on 2.4 GHz and is steered up to 5 GHz, and the old 2.4 GHz session is torn down exactly 30 seconds later. That single mechanism accounts for 176 of the day’s disconnect entries — every one of them routine housekeeping, not a fault.

Meraki draws all of that activity, so a perfectly healthy timeclock produces a dense, alarming-looking bar.

Correction to an earlier version of this document

An earlier draft said “another client at this store has done exactly the same thing every day since 24 August.” That was wrong on both counts, and the truth is more useful.

Checking the switch logs directly: the flapping began 13 August, not the 24th, and it occurred on 14 days out of 19 — frequent, but not daily. More importantly, the “other client” was almost certainly this same timeclock under its previous Wi-Fi address (see below), not a second device. The coverage problem is therefore older and more concentrated on this one spot than we first reported.

The real findingA coverage gap between two access points

The timeclock sits where it can hear POS AP reasonably well but BOH AP only faintly. So it keeps trying to roam to BOH AP, fails to hold the connection, and falls back — 47 times in a day.

CorroborationYour BOH switch logged MAC flaps between the two AP ports — the network seeing this device behind both access points at once.
How longSince 13 August — three weeks, on 14 separate days. This is a long-standing condition at this spot, not something that started recently.
Not unique to itOther devices on the staff Wi-Fi show as much join/leave churn or more. The location and the AP overlap are the common factor.

Nothing else in the store is implicated: no access point reboots, no channel changes, no uplink flaps, and the MX is clean. All six Meraki devices at the store are online.

Worth knowing: these timeclocks are invisible to Meraki, and that is a setting you control.

This iPad connects using an iOS “Private Wi-Fi Address” — a randomised MAC address rather than its real one. That is why Meraki shows no manufacturer and no operating system for it, and why its history only goes back to 1 September: the address is new, so the device looks new.

We watched it happen at this store. Until 1 September the timeclock was on the network as MAC 3a:19:87:3b:80:03 holding 192.168.1.59 — 354 address renewals between 6 August and 01:44 on 1 September. At 02:55 that morning it came back as a brand-new MAC, b6:41:19:a4:d8:fb, and took the next address, 192.168.1.60. The two never overlap; there is a 71-minute gap between the last lease of the old identity and the first of the new one.

To Meraki those look like two different devices. One physical iPad, two client records, two IP addresses, and a history that appears to begin on 1 September when the device has in fact been there since at least 6 August. The roaming problem above is only visible as a three-week pattern because we joined those two records back together by hand.

This is not one store. Nine of your ten timeclocks across the estate do the same thing. The practical consequences: you cannot identify these devices in Meraki, MAC-based allowlisting and per-device policy will not hold, and their connection history resets whenever the address rotates.

The fix is a single MDM setting — disable Private Wi-Fi Address for the corporate SSID on managed iPads. Valencia (store 65) is already configured this way and shows the desired end state, so this is a change you have proven once already.

UnresolvedWhat we could not determine

  • Meraki’s own labels for three of the four markers. We can see exactly what the device did at those moments, but Meraki’s failure classification sits behind a report we do not yet pull. The 07:04 handshake timeout is the one we can name with certainty.
  • Whether other devices failed at those same instants. That would settle “device” versus “access point” definitively. The evidence we do have points to the access-point/coverage side.
  • Device model and iOS version — hidden by the randomised address described above.

What we recommend

  • Leave the timeclock alone. It is working. No visit, no swap, no reboot needed.
  • Look at coverage between POS AP and BOH AP at this store. The flapping has been going on for three weeks and other staff devices show the same churn, so this is the fix with the widest benefit. Options range from adjusting transmit power and roaming thresholds to physically relocating an AP.
  • Turn off Private Wi-Fi Address on the managed iPads for the corporate SSID. This restores your ability to identify and govern them in Meraki, fleet-wide.
  • If you want the definitive per-failure breakdown, we can add Meraki’s failed-connections report to Argus so this chart is explained automatically next time rather than investigated by hand.

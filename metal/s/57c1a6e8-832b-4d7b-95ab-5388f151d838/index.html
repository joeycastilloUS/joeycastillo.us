S15 is a second class of runbook, not a setting

Two classes. They cannot share a DIA, and the library has to say which is which.

You already called this — "a new .pi and DIA that looks different than the one we have now." It is worth writing down why, because the difference is the safety floor itself, stated in the current runbook's own words:

D9   … the deterministic "We're Sorry" recovery, at zero cost …
     Same feedback in, same reply out, always.

D10  … No model ever writes a word a guest will read.
Deterministic — what we haveGenerative — S15
Who writes the guest's wordsThe runbook. Fixed text, vault-claimed code.The selected models, in full prose.
Same input twiceByte-identical reply, always.Not guaranteed. That is the point.
Cost per reply$0.00 — Tier-0, no modelModel cost per guest, every time.
What the models doJudge only — safe / not safe.Judge and author.
ReplayExact, LLM-free.Recorded, not reproducible.

To be exact about the cost row: the illness panel (D4) runs models on both classes and costs roughly a third of a cent per alert. The row is about who authors the reply and what that authoring costs — not a claim that the deterministic card never touches a model.

Neither is wrong. Deterministic is right when a wrong word is a liability — an illness path, a refund, a coupon. Generative is right when a canned reply is the insult — a guest who wrote three specific paragraphs and gets back a form letter. A shop needs both.

But they are not a toggle on one runbook. D9 and D10 are demands, and a runbook that lets a model author guest-facing prose is not the same card with a flag set — it is a card whose demands say something else. Hence: new .pi, new DIA, its own ~d.

The consequence for the surface we are building: once both classes exist in one library, the operator must be able to see at a glance which runbooks let a model speak to a guest. That is not decoration — it is the single most important fact about any runbook on the list. Proposal: a class badge on every card, and the detail view leads with it.

One thing the apply path must not quietly undo. Re-sealing is not bookkeeping — it is the approval act. The runtime says so itself: the seal is trust-on-first-use and “guards accidental edits” (dia.h:49), and an edited words block is refused with reseal to approve (dia.c:340). Today a human reseals, and that human is the gate. An applier that reseals automatically removes that gate — so from the moment 3.3 exists, the applier's own write-scope becomes the effective approval boundary. Stated as a fact of the mechanism, not a restriction on the operator: whatever the applier can write is what ships without a human reseal.

One note recorded, not argued: the mission is Tier-0 — $0.00, no model, deterministic from the start. A generative runbook is deliberately outside that floor. You have called it, and it is your call; it is written here so a future session does not read a generative card as a bug in the deterministic one.

Order of work — what stops when time runs out

Built in this order so that whenever we stop, what exists is complete and honest rather than half-wired:

Stop afterWhat you can demo

+ 1.3The full library, with sandbox/live state and demand counts. Cards are display-only — nothing invites a click that has nowhere to go.
+ 1.4Cards become openable, and open into the rules in the runbook's own words. This is the story.

The open affordance ships with the screen it opens, and the badge ships on the first rung, so no rung leaves a control that goes nowhere or real data wearing no label. Each rung is a place we can honestly stand.

Riskiest item

1.4 — making the rulebook legible without faking it. Unchanged from v1, and still the one that can go wrong. A rushed summarizer that misrenders your own policy is worse than showing nothing. De-risk: the verbatim text is always on screen and is the authority; anything friendlier sits beside it, never instead of it. If 1.4 runs long, ship the verbatim view alone — it fully satisfies S3 and costs 1 point instead of 3.

Unknowns

  • The library will show three cards, not the mockup's four. The retired autoresponse lives in the superseded directory the bake does not read. Adding it back is the "one line and a badge" above.
  • One seal file (mendo-nps-sweep.pi.seal) is zero bytes. A SEALED badge derived from file presence would be lying about it — the bake treats an empty seal as unsealed until someone confirms that file is intentional.
  • The design mockup draws Runbooks late in the tab row; the plan is authoritative — it goes immediately after Focus. Do not "fix" the code to match the picture.
  • Demo screen width. The console re-derives its layout below 640px and we have not seen the machine.
  • Whether safe-to-simulate and the retired autoresponse should appear as history. Default: not shown.
  • Whether the sealed-file details belong on screen or are engineer-only. Default: seal status only, no contents.
  • Whether the top-tab preference means this surface or the whole console. Answer arrives from your reaction to 1.7.
  • S14 retire vs disable — are they different states? Disable reads as "stop running it, keep it"; retire reads as "it is history." The current library already shows a retired card, so the distinction is probably real.
  • S14 enable/disable needs an owner in the runtime — something has to honor the flag. The sweep is the natural place, but it is a different card, so this is cross-runbook state.
  • S15's guardrails are undesigned — if a model authors guest prose, what still cannot be said? The illness floor presumably survives in some form. That question is the heart of its ~d.

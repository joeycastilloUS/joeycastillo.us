Runbooks — putting the rulebook in your hands

A new surface in Argus that shows every automated procedure you own, in plain language, and marks exactly which dials are yours to turn.

Status: designed, planned, estimated. One decision is holding up the build — it is at the bottom, and it is the most important part of this page.

The idea in one paragraph

Your system already runs on written rulebooks — one per procedure. Today they are files only an engineer opens. Runbooks puts them in Argus, next to where you already watch tickets: a list of every procedure you own, and inside each one, its promises in the words the rulebook itself uses, plus the settings grouped by who is allowed to change them — you, IT, or nobody.

Nothing on the screen is written by hand or made up for the demo. Every line is read out of the real rulebook files.

Pencil in a change
Penciled in — nothing has changed yet. The system runs from the sealed rulebook. A penciled change takes effect only when the owner applies it and the rulebook is re-sealed. Neither penciling-in nor the apply step is built yet. Both are next, in that order.

The "not built yet" line stays on the screen. It is the honest state, and it is also the safety property — see below.

Why "not built yet" is the right answer today

The system refuses to run on a rulebook that has been altered behind its back — that is deliberate, and it is the whole reason this is trustworthy. So the path is: pencil it in → the owner applies it → the rulebook re-seals → the system picks it up. The apply step is the next thing we build. Until it exists, the screen says so in words rather than pretending.

We have twice thrown away a version of this screen for showing controls that did nothing. That will not be on stage.

The decision that is holding this up

Argus's login is a curtain, not a lock.

While planning how to get the rulebooks onto the screen, we checked whether they would be protected once published. They would not be. Argus's sign-in hides the page; it does not guard files published beside it. So the rulebooks cannot go up the straightforward way.

That matters here because the rulebooks contain real operational detail — account identifiers, the people on the escalation list, and your company's name throughout — and they are marked confidential. Publishing them the straightforward way would put that on the open internet.

So, three ways forward:

OptionCostThe honest downside
A — publish with the sensitive parts removed+2The removed parts are exactly the settings this feature is about. The screen would look complete while hiding the point.
B — serve the rulebooks behind the password, properly+5The right answer. It is server work and cannot land tonight.
C — run tomorrow's demo from the local machine+1Not live on the public site that day. Everything on screen is still completely real.

Recommendation: C tomorrow, B this week. C leaks nothing, costs almost nothing, and does not compromise a single pixel of the demo. A is the one to avoid.

Open questions

  • A, B, or C for tomorrow.
  • Two different files share the same runbook name and hold different contents — which is the real one? The library will show two identically-named cards until that is settled.
  • Tabs across the top: this surface only for now, or the whole console later?

How this was produced

Every step ran as a pair — one model drives, a second independently checks and owns the verdict. Disagreements are surfaced, never quietly settled. The finding above is what that process is for.

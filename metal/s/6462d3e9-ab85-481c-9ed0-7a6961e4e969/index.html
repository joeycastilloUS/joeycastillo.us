Who owns what

The guest-recovery triage — a working agreement between Help Desk (Ops) and IT.
Prepared for Brendan Alexander, Help Desk Manager · and the IT team · currently running in the sandbox

What the system does, in five lines

  1. A guest leaves low-score NPS feedback → an alert ticket is created in Zendesk.
  2. Two AI models read the guest's own words and check for a possible food-borne illness.
  3. If either model has any doubt → it escalates to a human. The guest is never auto-contacted.
  4. If both agree it's safe → the guest gets a "We're Sorry" note and a coupon.
  5. Every message — to a guest or to your team — goes through Zendesk. Nothing else.

The principle that decides ownership

Ops owns what the guest and the escalation team experience — the words, the offer, the people. IT owns the machinery that makes contact correct, deduplicated, and contained. Anything whose wrong value could put a message in front of a possibly-ill guest — or move guest data outside our boundary — is Locked: neither side changes it alone.
The test for any future question: if this is wrong, does a guest see the wrong thing (Ops), does the system do the wrong thing (IT), or does a sick guest get a coupon (Locked)?

What "Locked" means in practice

Locked does not mean nobody is accountable. It means neither Ops nor IT may change it alone — and it comes in two kinds:

  • Locked invariants (D6, D15, and the "no AI writes guest text" rule): effectively unchangeable. Changing them changes what the product is — that's a design review, not a config request.
  • Locked parameters (D5's strictness, D14 arming, the D4 BYOK gate): changeable, but only in writing — one side proposes, the other reviews, leadership signs, and the change ships with an audit trail.

When Ops and IT disagree: the proposal goes in writing (a ticket, not a chat) → the counterpart reviews → leadership decides → it ships with a record. Until then, current behavior stands. For anything touching safety, the stricter interpretation stands by default — the system fails closed, and so does the way we govern it.

Three calls we're naming honestly

  • D5 — how strict the illness check is. This one will feel like an Ops decision. It isn't. Escalation volume will be high, most flags will look like false positives, and "can one model's OK be enough?" is a reasonable-sounding question. The day that softens is the day a guest who reported food poisoning receives an automated apology coupon — a guest-safety failure, and a permanent record that we auto-replied to an illness report. Locked, deliberately.
  • D14 — arming production. IT's hands flip it, Ops confirms the guest-facing content is real, leadership authorizes. Three signatures, on purpose.
  • D4 — BYOK models. Using outside AI providers would send guest words — including health claims — beyond our Google boundary. That is a legal and data-governance decision, not an IT preference. It stays gated until leadership and legal sign off.
The one mistake to avoid. Handing Ops the strictness knob (D5) because it looks like customer-care policy. Wrong words embarrass; a wrong D5 injures. Keep it Locked, and keep the lock visible, so pressure to loosen it arrives as a written proposal on a leader's desk — not as a quiet Tuesday config change.

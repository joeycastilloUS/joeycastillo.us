The card declares. Argus honors. The runtime stays free.

Move the configuration contract out of my Python and into the .pi, where it is declared intent — and let the app layer render from it.

Paired serial per the working agreement — driver writes, navigator reviews with authority to block. Operator decision 2026-07-21: the DIA runtime does not know about these attributes and does not enforce them; it executes the card as specced. This design is app layer only.

1 · The need

Right now, the most important fact on the screen is a dictionary I typed.

be/tools/bake-runbooks.py:40
OWNERSHIP = {
    "escalation.recipients":   "OPS",
    "panel.models":            "IT",
    "panel.fold":              "LOCKED",     <-- "the safety floor"
    ...
}

Who may change what — and which settings are the safety floor — is asserted by the baker, not declared by the card. Three consequences, all live today:

  • The card cannot say what it permits. D4 lists 34 approved models — 26 in-boundary plus 8 BYOK-gated; nothing in the card says whether a person may pick among them, or who that person is.
  • A new card gets nothing. CARDS is a hardcoded 3-element list and the ownership map is keyed by hand, which is exactly why two of three runbooks render "settings not yet mapped."
  • The truth is in the wrong repo. The intent lives in argus's build tooling rather than in the card, so the card is not actually the source of truth it claims to be.

2 · Raw user stories

As the operator, when I open a runbook I see a choice where the card permits a choice, and plain text where it doesn't — and I never have to ask an engineer which is which.

As the operator, the settings that are mine are mine because the card says so, not because someone configured a tool.

As the operator, when I add a new runbook it brings its own configurability with it.

As the operator, I am never shown a control that suggests the system will stop me when it won't.

3 · The seam — stated plainly, because it is the whole design

LayerRoleState
The .piDeclares what is configurable, by whom, within what boundsthe source of truth
Argus (bake + console)Honors the declaration — renders controls and ownership from itthis design
The DIA runtimeFree. Does not read, does not enforce. Executes the card as speccedunchanged, by decision

This is a real architecture, not a compromise: the card becomes the single place intent is written, and the app layer is the first consumer. When the runtime is ready to enforce, it reads the same declarations — nothing needs re-authoring.

And it costs nothing today. The runtime never parses these lines — there is no allow: branch and no struct field — and anything past byte 511 it never even sees. bake-runbooks.py has no such cap, so the app layer already sees everything. We are not working around the truncation; we are simply not blocked by it.

Worth knowing before step 6: adding declarations lengthens the clause text they sit in. D2 is 449 bytes and would cross 511 once its own example declarations are added. Declarations append after prose so truncation eats them first — but D4 already hides 182 bytes of prose from the runtime, including the very sentence that maps vertex-gemini to its real model name. That is the sharpest evidence that this truth is currently in the wrong place.

4 · The grammar — and one rule that decides everything

Declarations sit indented under the demand they serve, per the clarify design of record. Reading the card, the bound is next to the reason for the bound.

D4   Check every alert for a possible food-borne illness. The check is a
     panel of two models …

     allow:  panel.models = vertex/gemini-2.5-pro, vertex/gemini-2.5-flash, …
     allow:  panel.models requires="BYOK — guest data leaves the Google boundary" =
             byok/gpt-5.5, byok/o3, …
     allow:  panel.models alias vertex-gemini = vertex/gemini-2.5-flash
     editor: panel.models = it

D7   Escalate an illness by posting a PRIVATE note … Mark it urgent.

     allow:  escalation.priority = normal, high, urgent
     editor: escalation.priority = ops
     editor: escalation.recipients = ops
     editor: words.escalation-note = ops

D2   If this alert ticket already carries our handled-tag, stop …

     ignore: email-from = qa-test@example.com  until=2026-08-15

Two deliberate departures, flagged rather than smuggled. (1) The clarify design of record specifies only allow: and ignore: — editor: is new, and necessary once the ownership map dies, because something in the card must carry it. It is spelled editor: rather than owner: because owner: jc already exists at the top of every card meaning the card's owner; one keyword with two meanings distinguished only by indentation is a trap. (2) alias is new — see the out-of-set case below, which is not hypothetical.

The rule: undeclared means locked

If a setting carries no owner:, the console renders it read-only and labels it the safety floor. The card must grant; silence never grants.

This inverts today's posture, where my dictionary decides and anything I forgot to type simply vanishes from the UI. Under this rule a forgotten declaration fails closed — the operator sees the setting, sees it is locked, and asks. That is the correct direction for a mistake to point.

One thing this rule does NOT fix on its own: the two unmapped cards do not currently render locked settings — they render nothing, because the bake builds config for the first card only (bake-runbooks.py:473). Generalising that is its own step below, not a free consequence.

Every declared key must resolve, or it renders as an error

The bake cross-checks each declared key against the union of the .si's set: keys, the .pi's config: keys, and its words block names. An unknown key renders "declared but unbound" — never a control.

This rule exists because the first draft of this very document broke it: it used allow: coupon.amount = 10, 20, 30, 50 as its flagship example. There is no coupon.amount. The amount is the face value of a code claimed from the vault (D11); {amount} in the reply is a template slot filled at run time, not a setting. That example would have rendered a chooser wired to nothing — the exact failure this design is meant to prevent, in the design that proposes it. The navigator caught it; the rule is the fix.

Every control now carries the line that produced it. The screen can be checked against the card.

When the current value is outside the declared set — which is true today

This is not a hypothetical edge case. The plan runs set: panel.models = vertex-gemini, vertex-gemini-pro; the card permits vertex/gemini-2.5-flash-style names. Neither current value is a member of its own allow set. The only thing reconciling them is a prose sentence in D4 — and that sentence sits past byte 511, so it is machine-readable nowhere.

The rule: an out-of-set value renders amber, verbatim, labelled "in use, outside the declared set — not clamped." Never silently corrected, never hidden, and never drawn as a member. That matches the record's never-silently-clamp rule and it makes today's real mismatch visible instead of papering over it.

Two ways to actually resolve it, both cheap: an alias line in the card (machine-readable, keeps both vocabularies), or normalise the .si to the slash names. Pick one during step 6 — the mismatch is live right now.

Editing a words block is not like editing a dial

Granting editor: words.zora-reply = ops is correct — the voice is yours. But dia_verify check 4 is fail-closed on words drift: the moment an applied edit changes a words block, the live card refuses to load until it is resealed. So any future apply path must treat edit and reseal as one atomic act. A words grant without that coupling is a documented way to brick a running runbook — and the runbook currently fires every fifteen seconds.

Nothing to build today; this design only grants the declaration. Recorded so step 6 does not hand out words grants without knowing what they imply.

The honesty line this design must carry

A chooser that only offers permitted values looks like a guarantee. It is not one. Argus constrains what an operator types here; nothing constrains the system. The plan file can still name an unlisted model, and it would run — .si set: is never checked against the card's allow set.

So the pane says it once, plainly, at the top of settings:

These bounds are declared by the runbook and honored by this console.
The engine does not enforce them yet — a change made outside Argus
is not checked against them.

One sentence at the top is not enough — it scrolls away. Every control already carries the line that produced it, so that line carries the distinction too: declared by D7 · honored here, not enforced by the engine. "Declared by D7" alone reads as authority. The four extra words are what keep it from doing so.

Without both, this design quietly recreates the failure we have thrown out three times: a control that implies a power it does not have.

Steps 1–5 are mechanical. Step 6 is the real work, and it is a conversation about your intent, not a coding task — each of the 13 settings needs an owner and, where it is a choice, its permitted set.

Unknowns

  • Whether owner: should name a role (ops) or a person. Roles are right until someone other than you logs in — which S9's cancellation removed from scope.
  • Whether words.* blocks want an allow: at all, or are always free text when owned. Probably free text; a sentence has no enumerable set.
  • Whether a declaration can span cards (a house rule every runbook inherits) or is strictly per-card. Per-card until there is a second customer.
  • Resolved, not open: ignore: does not render until the engine honors it. It is the only statement whose whole meaning is a runtime behaviour — skip this ticket at intake — so drawing it would assert an effect that does not exist. Zero cards use it today, so deferring costs nothing. It stays in the grammar spec; it stays off the screen.

When to revisit

The moment the runtime starts enforcing — then the honesty sentence changes, and dia.c parses the same lines the bake already reads, with the seal extended to cover them. Also revisit if DiaClause.text is ever raised past 512, because at that point the runtime can see these lines and "does not enforce" becomes a choice rather than a consequence.
